# After the Word, thread 3: "To proclaim liberty to the captives"

**Status: proposed, waiting for Seabass.** Nothing here is built into the app until he approves it (SPIRIT §11).

**How this was made:** Claude researched it on 2026-10-19. Facts come from the sources listed under each entry. Where sources disagree, the entry says so rather than choosing. The verse each entry is tied to is **Claude's suggestion**, and none of these people is claimed to have quoted it unless a source says so. Verse text is BSB, copied from the app's own data.

The entries run in date order: Christians who spoke and worked against slavery, from the 4th century to today. The story is not all bright. Many Christians also owned and traded slaves, and these entries do not pretend otherwise. They follow the ones who heard the Word and set people free.

---

## 1. Gregory of Nyssa preaches against owning people (c. 380)
Preaching on Ecclesiastes 2:7, where the Teacher boasts that he bought slaves, Bishop Gregory of Nyssa asked the slave owner: "For how many obols did you value the image of God?" and "For how many coins did you sell this nature formed by God?" Because people are made in God's image, he argued, no price can buy one. The scholar Ilaria Ramelli calls him the only church father who completely denounced slavery as an indefensible evil.

- **Uncertain:** the homily's exact year is not known. Gregory died about 395. Scholars also disagree about whether the sermon was a call to free all slaves or a sermon on pride that went further than any other of its day.
- **Tied to:** "So God created man in His own image; in the image of God He created him;" (Genesis 1:27)
- **Sources:** [Gregory of Nyssa, Homily 4 on Ecclesiastes, tr. Andrew Maguire (Early Church Texts)](https://earlychurchtexts.com/public/gregoryofnyss_ecclesiastes_slavery.htm) · [Wikipedia, Gregory of Nyssa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_of_Nyssa)

## 2. Patrick, once a slave himself, demands the captives back (c. 450)
At sixteen, Patrick was seized from Britain by Irish raiders and held as a slave in Ireland for six years. He escaped, and later went back to Ireland to preach. When the soldiers of a ruler named Coroticus killed some of his newly baptised converts and carried others off as slaves, Patrick wrote an open letter cutting them off from the Church until they set the captives free. He asked that they "set free the baptised women prisoners whom they previously seized."

- **Uncertain:** Patrick's writings cannot be dated more exactly than the 5th century. The years of his captivity come from his own account, his *Confession*.
- **Tied to:** "The LORD sets the prisoners free," (Psalm 146:7)
- **Sources:** [Patrick, Letter to the soldiers of Coroticus, tr. McCarthy (confessio.ie)](https://www.confessio.ie/etexts/epistola_english) · [Wikipedia, Saint Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick)

## 3. Anselm's council forbids the slave trade in England (1102)
At a church council in London in 1102, Anselm, Archbishop of Canterbury, obtained a resolution against the slave trade in England, aimed mainly at the sale of English slaves to the Irish.

- **Honest note:** it did not stop the Church from owning slaves, and it did not end slavery in England.
- **Tied to:** "to set the oppressed free and tear off every yoke?" (Isaiah 58:6)
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, Council of London in 1102](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_London_in_1102) · [Internet Medieval Sourcebook, Anglo-Saxon slavery (Fordham)](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/angsax-slavery.asp)

## 4. Four Quakers in Germantown write the first protest (1688)
On 18 February 1688, Francis Daniel Pastorius and three other Quakers in Germantown, Pennsylvania, wrote a petition against slavery to their meeting. It was the first protest against slavery by a religious group in the English colonies. It rested on the Golden Rule, and it argued that every person has rights, whatever their belief, colour or people. The meetings above them called it too weighty to decide, and it was forgotten for about 150 years. Found again in 1844, it became a focus for abolitionists.

- **Uncertain:** the meeting said it would send the petition on to London, but there is no evidence that it arrived.
- **Tied to:** "In everything, then, do to others as you would have them do to you." (Matthew 7:12)
- **Sources:** [National Park Service, Germantown Quaker Petition Against Slavery](https://www.nps.gov/articles/quakerpetition.htm) · [Wikipedia, 1688 Germantown Quaker Petition Against Slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1688_Germantown_Quaker_Petition_Against_Slavery)

## 5. John Woolman's quiet persuasion (1754)
John Woolman, a Quaker, drafted *Some Considerations on the Keeping of Negroes* in 1746, and it was published in 1754. It became the most widely read essay against slavery by any Friend up to that time. He travelled as a minister, as far south as North Carolina, and he refused to write wills that handed slaves on to heirs. In the same year the Philadelphia Yearly Meeting sent out a letter condemning slavery. He died in 1772, and by the end of the American Revolution almost all North American Quakers had freed their slaves.

- **Uncertain:** some sources give 1753 for the essay. The yearly meeting's minutes do not name Woolman as an author of its letter, though one historian credits him.
- **Tied to:** "From one man He made every nation of men, that they should inhabit the whole earth;" (Acts 17:26)
- **Sources:** [Quakers and Slavery, "1754" (Bryn Mawr, Haverford and Swarthmore)](https://web.tricolib.brynmawr.edu/speccoll/quakersandslavery/commentary/themes/1754.php) · [Wikipedia, John Woolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Woolman)

## 6. Olaudah Equiano buys his freedom and tells his story (1766–1789)
Equiano, enslaved as a boy, was baptised at St Margaret's, Westminster, in 1759. In 1765 his owner, Robert King, a Quaker merchant, promised him his freedom for £40, the price King had paid for him. Equiano earned the money by trading, and bought his freedom. In London he joined the movement against the slave trade, and in 1789 published *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano*. It went through nine editions in his lifetime and is credited with helping turn opinion toward the Slave Trade Act of 1807.

- **Uncertain:** sources give 1766 or 1767 for his freedom. His book says he was born in Africa, but a baptism record and a ship's list say Carolina, and scholars still disagree.
- **Tied to:** "no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a beloved brother." (Philemon 1:16)
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, Olaudah Equiano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olaudah_Equiano) · [Documenting the American South, summary of the Narrative (UNC)](https://www.docsouth.unc.edu/neh/equiano2/summary.html)

## 7. William Wilberforce: twenty years, then the end (1787–1833)
Wilberforce, a member of Parliament, became an evangelical Christian in 1785. In 1787 he wrote in his diary: "God Almighty has set before me two great objects, the suppression of the slave trade and the reformation of manners." His motions failed in 1791 and stalled in 1792, and the campaign went on until the Slave Trade Act received royal assent on 25 March 1807. That ended the trade, but not slavery itself. The Slavery Abolition Act passed its third reading in the Commons on 26 July 1833, and Wilberforce died three days later.

- **Uncertain:** sources differ on how the dates of the 1833 Act's later stages line up with his death.
- **Tied to:** "He has sent Me to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18)
- **Sources:** [English Heritage, William Wilberforce blue plaque](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/blue-plaques/wilberforce-william/) · [Wikipedia, Slave Trade Act 1807](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807)

## 8. Harriet Tubman goes back for the others (1849–1862)
Harriet Tubman escaped slavery in 1849. She then went back about 13 times, guiding about 70 enslaved people to freedom, many of them her own family, and she gave directions to 50 or 60 more. People called her "Moses." She was a woman of deep faith, and in a letter she dictated she said: "God set the North Star in the heavens; He gave me the strength in my limbs; He meant I should be free."

- **Uncertain:** her birth year is not known. Some older accounts give much larger numbers of journeys and of people rescued, and the figures here are the more careful ones.
- **Tied to:** "Let My people go," (Exodus 5:1)
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, Harriet Tubman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Tubman) · [National Park Service, Harriet Tubman National Historical Park](https://www.nps.gov/hart/)

## 9. International Justice Mission (1997)
In 1997 the lawyer Gary Haugen founded International Justice Mission in Washington, D.C., a Christian organisation whose work includes fighting forced labour and slavery. Between 2006 and 2012, according to the U.S. State Department, it helped nearly 4,000 victims and helped prosecute 220 offenders. Slavery did not end in the 19th century, and this work goes on today.

- **Uncertain:** the figures are from 2006–2012. Later totals from IJM itself were not checked.
- **Tied to:** "seek justice and correct the oppressor." (Isaiah 1:17)
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, International Justice Mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Justice_Mission) · [International Justice Mission](https://www.ijm.org/)
